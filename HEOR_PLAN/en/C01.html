Module 1 · Planning for health: what it is, who does it, in what context

Who decides how many beds Romania has? Planning, its levels, and the social and health system context

Module objectives

§By the end of the module, you will be able to:

  • explain what planning is and what a plan is, how health services planning differs from planning in other fields, why it always has a political dimension, who needs it, why it is resisted and what time horizon suits it;
  • distinguish national planning, community planning (for a county or a region) and planning at the level of the organisation, and recognise, in the Romanian system, who carries out each type of planning and with what instruments;
  • describe the context in which planning takes place: the sociocultural framework, demographic trends, the attitudes of service users and the transformation of the health system in Romania;
  • explain the shift from the medical care paradigm to the population health paradigm and to population health management, together with their barriers;
  • read hospital capacity indicators critically (beds per 100,000 population, discharges, average length of stay, avoidable hospitalisations) and argue why a system with many beds can have unmet needs;
  • defend, from an institutional role, a position in a hospital network planning decision and recognise who bears the cost of each option.

Preparing for the module

§Before the module, read chapter 1 of the course textbook (Thomas, Health Services Planning, third edition), which corresponds to the first topic, and go through the section on hospitals in Romania: Country Health Profile, published by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies1. Then write down, in one sentence, your answer to the question in the title: who actually decides how many beds there are in the hospital where you work, or in the one you know best? You will compare your answer with the vote of all participants in the first hour.

§The module lasts four hours and follows a single real case, Romania's hospital network and beds, in three acts, with a break between them. You will be working for about half of the time: individual votes on your phone, with the result shown on screen after the vote closes, a role-play committee in which each participant speaks on behalf of an institution, and a final transfer decision. You need a phone or a laptop with internet access.

§The outline, in brief:

  • Act 1 (the situation): how many beds Romania has compared with the European Union, who has a say over them and what “planning” actually means; the first two decisions;
  • Act 2 (the complication): the data that do not fit the picture of “we have enough beds”, the levels of planning and the social, demographic and institutional context of the Romanian system; the third decision and the role-play committee;
  • Act 3 (the resolution): what actually happened to the bed reductions and to the regional hospitals, the population health paradigm and its barriers; the transfer decision, what you will do differently on Monday morning and the exit ticket.

§There are three new terms we use most often. The first is the plan as a “framework for decision” (not as a document for the shelf). The second is the regional master plan for health services, that is, the plan that translates the needs of a region into services, staff and beds. The third is population health management: care organised for a defined group of people, not only for the patient who has reached the hospital. The rest of the vocabulary is explained in these notes.

The module case: Act 1

§In 2024, Romania had 730.7 hospital beds per 100,000 population, while the European Union (EU-27) average was 507.42. Of the states compared here, only Bulgaria had more, with 870.3, while Germany had 758.63. The National Health Strategy in force provides, among other things, for a revised national bed plan4, and three regional emergency hospitals are under way: those in Iași and Craiova are under construction, and in Cluj-Napoca the works contract was signed in May 2026567. At the same time, Romania is among the Union's states with the most hospital admissions that could have been avoided through good care outside the hospital, particularly for diabetes8.

§The case is real: the figures and documents cited have their source in the course register. The question we examine over the four hours has three parts: who decides how many beds Romania has, on what data they decide, and why a system with many beds can leave needs unmet.

§The first decision, which you will vote on at the start of the module, concerns an ordinary county hospital: who has the last word on its number of beds?

The committee role cards

§In the second act, the participants become the committee that discusses a proposal to reorganise the beds in a region. Each participant, in the room or on Microsoft Teams, receives a role according to the first letter of their surname: A–C, Ministry of Health; D–F, National Health Insurance House; G–I, county hospital manager; J–M, president of the county council; N–R, family doctor; S–Z, patients' association (letters with diacritics go with the base letter: Ă and Â with A, Î with I, Ș with S, Ț with T). The card for each role is one of those below; you also receive them in a document posted on Teams on the day of the module. Read all of them before the module: you will better understand what the others say. The proposal under discussion is constructed for the course, on the real data of the case; the roles describe institutions, not individuals.

Role card: Ministry of Health

§You are pursuing the implementation of the National Health Strategy: fewer acute care beds, more post-acute care, rehabilitation and palliative care beds, and services integrated across levels. You approve, by ministerial order, the bed plan of each county9. You fear that a new regional hospital will be added to the existing beds instead of reorganising them, and that the reductions will be reversed locally, as after past hospital reorganisations. Only you know how far the revision of the national bed plan has progressed and what conditions the investment funders attach. Your sentence in the committee: “The regional hospital is not one more hospital, but the apex of a network that must be rearranged.”

Role card: National Health Insurance House

§You pay for services from the single fund: the county insurance houses contract the contractable beds with the hospitals9 and must contract with all providers that meet the conditions10. You want the money to go to the services with the greatest benefit and avoidable hospitalisations to fall. You fear a reorganisation that shifts costs onto the fund without reducing anything: new rehabilitation beds paid for on top of the acute care beds that are kept. Only you know how much avoidable hospitalisations cost, by county, and how little the insurance houses can negotiate with providers. Your sentence: “We contract services, not buildings; show us what changes for the patient.”

Role card: county hospital manager

§You run a large county hospital in the region, with acute wards that are full in some months and empty in others. You want to preserve the hospital's revenue, staff and competences. You fear that the regional hospital will attract the complex cases and the best doctors, leaving your hospital with the cheap cases and reduced beds. Only you know how many of the hospital's admissions are patients who could have been treated as outpatients or at home, and how hard it is to discharge an older person who has no care after discharge. Your sentence: “I will not close beds until I see where the patients go the next day.”

Role card: president of the county council

§The county council owns the county hospital and answers to the voters. You want the county not to lose services, jobs and prestige, and to attract investment. You fear the press headline “Beds cut at the county hospital” and the protest of the staff. Only you know what investments the council has promised the hospital and how heavily its budget depends on transfers from central government. Your sentence: “I accept the reorganisation if the money saved stays in the county.”

Role card: family doctor

§You work in a rural commune in the county, with a large list of older patients with chronic diseases. You want to be able to care for your patients with diabetes, heart failure or chronic lung disease without them ending up in the emergency department (UPU). You fear that the reorganisation will move the beds but not the resources you need: time, laboratory tests, community nurses, rapid access to a specialist. Only you know how many of your patients were admitted for a decompensation that could have been prevented. Your sentence: “The cheapest bed is the one my patient does not need.”

Role card: patients' association

§You represent patients with chronic diseases and their families. You are concerned with access: the distance to the hospital, the waiting time, the continuity of care after discharge. You fear that a reorganisation decided without patients will lengthen journeys and leave families to care alone, at home, for a dependent older relative. Only you know what patients say about hurried discharges and about the journeys to a hospital with specialists. Your sentence: “Nothing about us without us: show us the map of services, not only the number of beds.”
