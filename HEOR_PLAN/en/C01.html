Module objectives

§By the end of the module, you will be able to:

  • explain what planning is and what a plan is, how health services planning differs from planning in other fields, why it always has a political dimension, who needs it, why it is resisted and what time horizon suits it;
  • distinguish national planning, community planning (for a county or a region) and planning at the level of the organisation, and recognise, in the Romanian system, who carries out each type of planning and with what instruments;
  • describe the context in which planning takes place: the sociocultural framework, demographic trends, the attitudes of service users and the transformation of the health system in Romania;
  • explain the shift from the medical care paradigm to the population health paradigm and to population health management, together with their barriers;
  • read hospital capacity indicators critically (beds per 100,000 population, discharges, average length of stay, avoidable hospitalisations) and argue why a system with many beds can have unmet needs;
  • defend, from an institutional role, a position in a hospital network planning decision and recognise who bears the cost of each option.

Act 1: a system with many beds

Case and first decision

Case studyWho decides how many beds Romania has, Act 1

§A real case; the figures and acts cited have their source in the course register. Romania has more hospital beds per 100,000 population than almost all the states of the Union: 730.7 in 2024, against an EU-27 average of 507.42. The ratio between the two values is 1.44: for the same population, Romania has almost half as many beds again as the European average11.

§The National Health Strategy for 2023–2030 (Strategia națională de sănătate), approved by Government Decision No. 1004/2023, provides for the restructuring of hospital services, with investments complementary to the construction of the three regional hospitals in Cluj-Napoca, Iași and Craiova12. The three regional emergency hospitals, in Iași, Craiova and Cluj-Napoca, are under way567. The practical question remains: when a county hospital wants to change its number of beds, who has the last word?

Applied activity 20 min

§First decision (individual vote): who actually has the last word on the number of beds of a public county hospital in Romania? (A) the Ministry of Health; (B) the health insurance house, through the beds it contracts; (C) the county council, as owner of the hospital; (D) none of them alone: the decision is shared among several institutions.

§The documented answer is option D, and the reason lies in the way the Romanian system is built. Public hospitals are, for the most part, administered by local authorities; only 58 hospitals are administered by the Ministry of Health13. The organisational structure of a hospital is proposed by the manager and approved, as the case may be, by the Ministry of Health or by the local authority that administers it14; hospitals in the local authorities' network are established and dissolved by Government decision, initiated by the prefect or by the county council, with the approval of the Ministry of Health15. The health insurance houses conclude contracts with hospitals for the number of contractable beds, within the limit of the bed plan approved for each county by order of the minister of health, in accordance with the National Bed Plan (Planul național de paturi)9, which the framework contract describes as approved by Government decision16; they must contract with all providers that meet the conditions, and volumes are negotiated within the limits of the budget10. The Ministry of Health also sets the direction: the national strategy provides for the revision of the national bed plan4 and for the development of hospital infrastructure in line with the regional master plans17. Option A overestimates the ministry: it approves the bed plan of each county, but it does not decide alone the structure of the hospitals it does not administer, nor which beds are contracted within the limit of this plan. Each of the three institutions can block or fund a change, but none of them can decide it alone. Option B is tempting, because the contracted beds are the ones that bring in money; but a contracted bed does not exist if the owner does not maintain it, and a maintained bed is not paid for if it is not contracted.

§This division of the decision is exactly the situation that the course textbook describes as specific to health planning: many actors, each with a share of the resources and with its own objectives18. To judge the case, you first need a few basic notions: what planning is, what a plan is, and what makes them different in health.

What planning is and what a plan is

§In a general sense, planning is the process by which a community or an organisation builds a coordinated and comprehensive mechanism for allocating resources to objectives specified in advance19. The definition has three elements, each with a practical consequence. “Process” means that planning does not end when a document is drafted. “Coordinated and comprehensive” means that decisions about beds, staff, equipment and money are taken together, not separately by each ward or institution. “Specified objectives” means that, at the end of the process, someone can say whether the objective has been achieved.

§The plan is the result of this process, but the textbook insists that the emphasis must be on the process, not on the document: the plan evolves constantly, it is usually revised before it is published, and some of its objectives are sometimes achieved before the text is finalised20. Hence the formula we use throughout the course: the plan is a framework for decision. It does not give the solution to every problem, but the mechanism through which the problems that arise are resolved, supported by explicit decision criteria21. In the course, we add to the criteria the indicators that are monitored and the points at which the decision is reviewed, without which the criteria cannot be applied.

§The usual reaction to the word “plan”, in health as in other fields, is that “the last plan is gathering dust on the shelf”22. The textbook treats this as a confusion about what a plan is and is not. A plan that has no responsible parties, deadlines and indicators is not a weak plan, but a declaration of intent. In the Romanian system, the difference can be seen by comparing a strategy that lists objectives with an action plan that says who carries out each measure, by when and how the result is measured; the National Health Strategy for 2023–2030 has both parts, and the action plan names, for each measure, the responsible institutions, the deadline and the indicator4.

Definitionhealth services planning

§The process by which a community, a region or an organisation establishes which health services the population it serves needs, with what resources and over what period, and builds the mechanism through which decisions are taken, monitored and reviewed against explicit objectives1920.

How planning in health differs

§Health services planning borrows the methods of planning from other fields, but applies them in a sector with distinctive features18. The textbook describes the American system; the features are also found in Romania, with the differences you see in this case. The first is the large number of actors with different objectives: the same decision about beds concerns patients, doctors, hospital managers, the payer, local authorities and the ministry, and each defines success differently. The second is fragmentation: services are provided by organisations that do not have a single owner and do not answer to the same authority, as you saw in the first decision. The third is third-party payment: the person who receives the service, the patient, is not the one who pays for it, the insurance house, and the one who recommends it, the doctor, is neither of them. The fourth is that many decisions concern life-and-death situations, which gives planning an emotional charge that other sectors do not have18.

§For the planner, the consequence of these features is that a good technical plan is not enough. It has to align actors who are not obliged to follow it. In Romania, for example, the health insurance house must contract with all providers that meet the standards and cannot selectively choose the hospitals it works with10. This means that the payer cannot use the contract as the main instrument for reorganising the network.

The political nature of planning

§Ideally, planning would be an objective process, driven by technical considerations. In practice, it always expresses someone's idea of how things should be; even a plan made by consensus is the product of one particular group and not of another23. The textbook adds a simpler reason why planning has a political dimension: plans are rarely made for the sake of planning. Something triggers the process: a crisis in publicly funded care, an epidemic, rising costs, and, at the level of an organisation, the actions of competitors or changes in the external environment23.

§The case of beds in Romania illustrates both ideas. The regional hospitals entered the public agenda through a 2004 hospitals law, which provided for the construction of eight regional hospitals and which was never adopted by Parliament; three of them were later taken up again in the regional operational programme for 2014–202024. The 2011 hospital reorganisation, with the closure of 67 public hospitals, started from the finding that there were too many acute care beds and aimed to convert them into long-term care beds25. Each of these decisions had a technical justification, but its timing, scale and fate depended on the political agenda; the health system review notes that political instability and insufficient administrative capacity often hinder the implementation of policies26.

§Recognising the political nature of planning does not mean giving up rigour. It means using rigour differently: data and explicit criteria make visible who wins and who loses from a decision, and a decision that contradicts the data has to be justified publicly. The planner cannot replace the political decision-maker, but can make that decision-maker's choice informed and owned.

Who needs planning and why it is necessary

§Almost all actors in the health system need planning: communities, which need to know which services they lack; organisations, from the county hospital to the family medicine practice; payers; the public authorities responsible for the health of the population27. What differs is the question each of them asks. The community asks which services its population needs, the organisation asks which services it can offer sustainably, and the payer asks which services deserve funding.

§The textbook argues for the need for planning through some of the consequences of its absence: resources used inefficiently, services duplicated in one place and absent in another, decisions taken either too late or impulsively, under the pressure of a crisis28. Without a framework, decisions tend either towards paralysis or towards reactions of the moment. In our case, the acute care beds that grew again after the 2011 reduction are an example of local decisions, each rational on its own, which, added together, contradict the declared direction of the system2925.

Why planning is resisted

§Although its advantages seem obvious, planning meets resistance, and the textbook explains why. One reason is that planning introduces accountability: the research underpinning the plan examines existing practices, and the implementation timetable assigns tasks and responsible parties30. Anyone who has worked in an institution knows that becoming measurable is uncomfortable. The second reason, specific to the United States, is a paradoxical cultural attitude towards centralised planning, associated with state intervention30. In Romania, resistance also takes other forms: distrust of plans that were not followed by money, fear of job losses in a small hospital and the frequent turnover of management teams, which shortens the horizon of decisions. These latter are observations of context, not the results of a study; we use them as hypotheses to be discussed in the committee.

The time horizon

§How far into the future does a plan look? The textbook answers that there is no single answer and proposes thinking in three horizons, short, medium and long, with at least five years as the minimum period to be considered for health services planning31. The reason is practical: an investment in hospital infrastructure is designed, contracted and built over several years. The Iași Regional Emergency Hospital has, on the project page, a completion date of 12 December 20296, and the works contract for the Cluj hospital provides for an execution period of 40 months7. A bed plan conceived over two years cannot take account of a hospital that opens in five.

§The long horizon has a price, however: the further we look, the more uncertain the assumptions about population, technology and funding become. That is why a good plan has review points: moments fixed in advance at which it is checked whether the assumptions still hold.

The second decision: which plan goes on the table first

Applied activity 20 min

§Second decision (individual vote): a region has to decide how many beds, and of what type, it needs after a regional hospital opens. Which document should be on the table first? (A) the management plan of each hospital in the region; (B) the regional master plan for health services, built on the assessment of the population's needs; (C) the National Health Strategy; (D) the insurance house's budget for the following year.

§The documented answer is option B. The national strategy sets the direction, but it does not say how many rehabilitation beds a particular region needs; the management plan of a hospital concerns a single organisation; the annual budget has the shortest horizon. The regional master plan is the document that starts from the assessment of care needs and sets regional targets for staffing, for the volume of services and for the number of beds by type of care32. The strategy itself requires hospital infrastructure to be developed in line with the regional master plans17. Option C is not wrong, but incomplete: without its regional translation, a national strategy cannot be applied to a specific hospital.

§This decision opens the second act: if the right document exists, why has the number of beds not changed in the desired direction?

Act 2: many beds, unmet needs

Case: the data that do not fit

Case studyWho decides how many beds Romania has, Act 2

§A real case; the figures have their source in the course register. If Romania has so many beds, one might think that its patients easily find the care they need. The data say otherwise. Hospital admissions that could have been avoided through good outpatient care and care from the family doctor, for asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, for heart failure and for diabetes, have long been among the highest in the Union. In 2023, Romania exceeded the European average for most of these conditions and had the highest rate for diabetes8.

§Rehabilitation beds are few: 72.4 per 100,000 population in 2024, against 192.4 in Germany and 195.0 in Poland33. Beds are unevenly distributed: from 542.2 per 100,000 population in the South-Muntenia region to 1,056.9 in the Bucharest-Ilfov region34. According to a 2020 study, over 80% of residents could reach a hospital by car within 30 minutes, but some areas, such as the Danube Delta and isolated mountain areas, remain hard to reach35.

The third decision: why many beds do not cover the needs

Applied activity 20 min

§Third decision (individual vote): what best explains the fact that a system with many beds has high avoidable hospitalisations and unequal access to specialised care? (A) too few doctors; (B) the beds are of the wrong type and in the wrong place, and care outside the hospital is weak; (C) patients overuse the hospital; (D) the total funding of the system is too low.

§The documented answer is option B, and the other options describe factors that are real but secondary for this question. The European Observatory describes the Romanian system as hospital-centred, with a large bed capacity, and patients seeking rapid care often bypass primary care and go directly to hospital38. The high avoidable hospitalisations show that some of the beds are used for care which, organised differently, would have taken place outside the hospital8. The small number of rehabilitation beds and the concentration of beds in certain regions show that the problem is not the total number, but the structure of the network3334. The National Health Strategy makes the same diagnosis: according to the European Observatory, it provides for better planning of services in order to reduce avoidable hospitalisations, the reclassification of hospitals and the resizing of the national number of beds, with some beds moved from acute care to long-term care39.

§Option C attributes the problem to patients. It contains some truth, but it reverses the cause: when the care of chronic diseases depends heavily on the higher levels of care, the patient goes to hospital because that is where the care is found8. Options A and D concern resources that matter, but which, added to a network with the same structure, would produce the same avoidable hospitalisations.

Discharges and length of stay: what we can and cannot compare

§Two indicators complete the picture of capacity. Discharges, that is, the number of patients who leave hospital after an admission, measure the volume of activity; the average length of stay measures how many days, on average, an admitted patient stays. Together they show how intensively a bed is used.

§In 2019, before the pandemic, Romania had 21,083.9 discharges per 100,000 population, and in 2021 it had 12,967.740. The fall, of 38.5%41, took place in the years of the COVID-19 pandemic; in the OECD series cited by the health system review, Romania's discharge rate fell in 2020 to 130 per 1,000 population, below the Union average of 14442. The average length of stay was 7.3 days in 2019 and 7.8 days in 202143. Eurostat publishes these indicators for Romania only up to 2021, in all four datasets checked (hlth_co_disch2, hlth_co_disch2t, hlth_co_inpst, hlth_co_inpstt), without the EU-27 average4043. For the years after the pandemic, the European comparison comes from another series, that of the OECD, cited by the health system review: in 2022, Romania had 162 discharges per 1,000 population, above the average of 26 Union states, of 15542. In 2023 it had the third-highest bed rate in the Union, together with high discharge rates44.

§Note one point that matters for planning: between 2019 and 2021, discharges fell by more than a third, but beds per 100,000 population increased, from 705.8 to 720.6241. Even in a period when activity fell sharply, beds did not follow it: they depend on organisational and funding decisions. A bed, once created, is hard to close: after the 2011 reorganisation, more than half of the closed hospitals reopened25.

Common misinterpretation

§A claim to check: “Romania has too many beds, so it must reduce all of them in the same proportion.”

§What the data show: Romania has more beds than the Union average for almost all types of care, but fewer rehabilitation beds than Germany or Poland, and the regional distribution is very unequal363334. A uniform reduction would deepen the shortages where beds are already few.

§General principle: an aggregate indicator, such as beds per 100,000 population, describes average capacity, not need. The planning decision is taken by type of care, by region and on the needs of the population served, not on the national average.

An order-of-magnitude calculation

Worked exampleHow many beds the gap with the European average represents

§The calculation uses real data and answers a simple question: if Romania had the European average bed rate, how many beds would it have? First in words: the number of beds is the rate per 100,000 population multiplied by the population, divided by 100,000.

§Step 1. The resident population of Romania on 1 January 2024 was 19,067,576, a value estimated by Eurostat45. Step 2. At Romania's 2024 rate of 730.7 beds per 100,000 population, this gives about 139,300 beds46. Step 3. At the EU-27 average rate of 507.4, it would give about 96,700 beds, that is, approximately 42,600 fewer beds46.

§The calculation is only an order of magnitude: the Eurostat rate refers to the average population of the year, not to that on 1 January, and the actual number of beds is read from the statistics of the National Institute of Statistics. Its conclusion is not that Romania should abolish 42,600 beds, but that the gap is so large that it cannot be explained by the needs of the population alone46.

How the number of beds a region needs is judged

§What would a data-driven decision about beds look like? The basic formula starts from activity, not from the existing beds. In words: the beds needed are the bed-days that the population will generate in a year, divided by the number of days in the year and by the occupancy rate we consider acceptable.

Worked examplehypothetical valuesThe acute care beds of Region A

§Hypothetical values. Region A has 2,500,000 inhabitants and an admission rate of 160 per 1,000 population per year, hence 400,000 admissions. The average length of stay is 7 days, and the target occupancy rate is 85%.

§Step 1. Bed-days: 400,000 × 7 = 2,800,000 days per year. Step 2. Beds occupied on average each day: 2,800,000 / 365 = 7,671 beds. Step 3. Beds needed at an occupancy rate of 85%: 7,671 / 0.85 = 9,025 beds, that is, 361 per 100,000 population.

§Now change a single assumption. If better care outside the hospital avoided 20% of admissions, 7,220 beds would be needed. If the average length of stay fell to 6 days, 7,736 beds would be needed. If both happened, 6,189 beds would be needed. The formula is the same; the decision about beds is, in fact, a decision about admissions and their length.

§The example shows why the question “how many beds?” is the wrong question if it comes first. The number of beds results from three prior choices: how many admissions the system accepts (and therefore how good care outside the hospital is), how long an admission lasts (and therefore which services exist after discharge) and what occupancy rate is targeted (and therefore how much reserve is kept for peaks in activity and for emergencies). Each choice belongs to a different level of planning.

The levels of planning

National planning

§The textbook distinguishes three levels of health services planning: planning at the national level, planning at the community level and planning at the level of the organisation47. They answer different questions, have different horizons and are carried out by different actors.

§National planning sets the directions for the whole system: public health priorities, the structure of the network, the funding of major investments. The textbook shows that, in most societies, planning starts at the national level and works its way down through the health system, but that the United States is an exception. There, certificate-of-need requirements, that is, the prior approval of an investment or a new service, still constrain the actions of private providers in some communities today48. For Romania, the appropriate point of comparison is a different one: in most industrialised countries, health planning is integrated into broader social planning49.

§In Romania, the national-level planning document is the National Health Strategy for 2023–2030, approved by Government Decision No. 1004/2023, having regard to the provisions of Law No. 95/2006 on health reform (Legea privind reforma în domeniul sănătății)1250. For hospitals, the strategy provides for a mechanism for planning and monitoring hospital infrastructure at the national level, with tiers of access at different levels51. Its action plan includes the revision of the national bed plan, with a deadline between the third quarter of 2024 and the fourth quarter of 2025, under the responsibility of the Ministry of Health, the National Institute for Health Services Management, the public health directorates, the local authorities and the providers with beds4.

§The European Observatory finds, however, that in Romania planning capacity is insufficient, which is officially acknowledged, and that the creation of a planning structure within the Ministry of Health has been a priority52. National planning exists better on paper than in institutions.

Community planning: the county and the region

§Community planning concerns a geographically defined population: a city, a county, a region47. Its question is which services this population needs and how they are divided among providers. The textbook observes that the success of community planning is measured, more often than not, by the health status of the community as a whole53.

§In Romania, the community level of hospital planning is mainly the development region. Since 2016, the Ministry of Health has drawn up regional plans and master plans based on the assessment of care needs, with regional targets for staff, the volume of services and the number of beds by type of care32. The first regional plans were a precondition for access to the European structural and investment funds of the 2014–2020 period; they were updated for the 2021–2027 period, and the assessment of health needs is the responsibility of the National Institute for Health Services Management, together with the National Institute of Public Health32. Three regional master plans were updated before the construction of the three regional hospitals, and the other five were finalised in 2023, through a project funded by the European Commission to strengthen planning capacity32.

§Note where the impetus came from: not from a Romanian planning law, but from a condition set by the European funder. This is one more example of the political nature of planning: the plan appeared when it was needed in order to obtain money23.

Planning at the level of the organisation

§Planning at the level of the organisation concerns a single provider: a hospital, a network of clinics, an ambulance service, an insurance house47. It answers the question of which services the organisation can offer, for which market and with what resources, and the textbook covers it at length, with its types (strategic, business and marketing planning), which you study in Modules 3 and 4.

§The tension between the level of the organisation and the level of the community is at the centre of our case. For a county hospital, keeping its acute care beds is a rational decision: they bring in revenue and justify the staff and competences. For the region, the same beds may be a surplus that consumes resources needed for rehabilitation or palliative care. Good community planning does not ignore the interest of the organisation, but makes it visible and negotiable.

Why planning is necessary in Romania

§From what you have seen so far, the reasons why planning is a necessity in Romania, not a theoretical exercise, can be brought together. The hospital network has more beds than the European average, but few rehabilitation beds and an unequal distribution23334. Avoidable hospitalisations point to hospital-dependent care for chronic diseases8. The decision on beds is shared among owners, the payer and the ministry1310. Major investments, such as the regional hospitals, are made over horizons of many years6. And, not least, the European funders made funds conditional on the existence of regional plans32. Each of these reasons calls for a coordinated decision-making mechanism, which is exactly what we defined as planning.

The context of planning: society, population, service users, the system

The sociocultural framework

§All planning takes place in a social and cultural context that the planner must understand before proposing anything. The textbook shows that the way planning is done depends on society's attitudes towards planning in general and towards government involvement in particular54. The values of a society decide which services are considered necessary: a society that values individual autonomy will demand different services from one that values care within the family.

§For Romania, one practical consequence, formulated as a hypothesis of the course and not as the result of a study, concerns the care of older people. When families are smaller and many of their members have left to work in other countries, the care of a dependent older person can no longer be left to the family alone; without care services after discharge, that person remains in the hospital bed. The geographical distribution adds a dimension of its own: in 2023, almost 91% of public hospitals were in urban areas, although 48% of the population lived in rural areas35.

Demographic trends

§In the textbook, demographic change is the driver of the epidemiological transition: an ageing population moves from a pattern of disease dominated by acute conditions to one dominated by chronic and degenerative diseases55. In this course, we start from the change in the age structure.

§Romania is following the same direction. The share of people aged 65 and over rose from 19.3% in 2021 to 20.3% in 2025, values estimated by Eurostat; in the Union, the share was 22.0% in 202556. The median age of the population reached 44.1 years, against 44.9 years in the Union, and the old-age dependency ratio, that is, the number of people aged 65 and over per 100 people aged 15–64, was 31.6, against 34.5 in the Union, in the same year56. Romania's population fell from 19,201,662 on 1 January 2021 to 19,043,151 on 1 January 202545.

§For bed planning, these figures have two consequences. First, the demand for care for chronic diseases, for rehabilitation and for palliative care will grow, even if the total population falls. Second, a smaller and older population does not need more acute care beds, but different services. You will work on projecting demand from the population in Module 2.

The attitudes of service users

§The third component of the context is the way people view health and services57. The textbook acknowledges that consumer attitudes are complex, but treats them as part of the context that the planner must take into account57. These attitudes change planning: a new service that does not take account of users' expectations may remain unused, as you will see in Module 3.

§For Romania, data on patient experience are scarce. In the 2016 European Quality of Life Survey, the quality of health services reported by patients in Romania was below the Union average, both for family doctors and health centres (7.1 against an average of 7.3) and for hospital and specialist services (6.2 against 6.9)58. In an electronic questionnaire of the Ministry of Health, in February 2025, 21% of the discharged respondents were dissatisfied or very dissatisfied with the hospital's services, at a response rate of 14.3%58. The low response rate means that the figure cannot be generalised; it does show, nevertheless, that there is a data source the planner can request.

The transformation of the health system in Romania

§The textbook concludes its description of the context with the transformation of the American health system, which it describes at length59. For Romania, the point of comparison is its own history. After several decades in which the system followed the centralised Semashko model, social health insurance was introduced in 199960. The system is organised on two main levels, national and county, but remains highly centralised60.

§The hospital network has also been transformed. The number of hospitals rose from 447 in 2002 to 554 in 2023, and the increase is due to private hospitals, from five in 2002 to 180 in 2023; of the 554 hospitals, 374 were public61. The closure of 67 public hospitals in 2011 temporarily reduced the total number from 503 in 2010 to 464 in 2011, but in the same year 97 small private hospitals opened, and more than half of the closed hospitals later reopened25.

§The transformation also has an unfinished part. The current strategy provides that new services, such as post-acute care services, should be developed in tandem with the restructuring of the acute care bed network, according to morbidity profiles and the need for services, and that the discharge of patients who need post-acute care should be ensured through dedicated services62.

The role-play committee: the proposal for a region

Applied activity 40 min

§Fourth decision, the module's tool: the role-play committee. The proposal, constructed for the course on the real data of the case: after the regional emergency hospital opens, some of the acute care beds of the county hospitals in the region are converted, over three years, into rehabilitation, post-acute care and palliative care beds, and part of the savings funds the care of chronic diseases in outpatient settings and by the family doctor. Each participant votes from their assigned role (Ministry of Health, National Health Insurance House, county hospital manager, president of the county council, family doctor, patients' association): (A) accepts the proposal as it stands; (B) accepts it with conditions (phasing, linking it to the regional master plan, changing the contracts); (C) rejects it. A spokesperson from two or three roles explains, in at most one minute, the vote of their role.

§The committee has no correct answer in advance; it has a closing question: which decision would pass, and who would bear its cost? If most roles choose option B, acceptance with conditions, the conditions say more than the vote. The ministry asks that the new beds not be added to the old ones; the insurance house asks that the change be visible in the contracts and in avoidable hospitalisations; the manager asks that patients have somewhere to go after discharge; the county council asks that the savings stay in the county; the family doctor asks for resources, not only for more patients; the patients' association asks to be consulted. Each condition is legitimate, and together they explain why reorganising the network takes years.

§The cost of such a decision is unequally distributed. The county hospital loses revenue and prestige in the short term; staff must be retrained or moved; patients gain in the long term, but during the transition period they may have longer journeys. Test one hypothesis against your vote: the decision that passes is the one whose cost falls on the actor with the weakest voice in the committee. The planner who knows this can propose compensatory measures before that actor blocks the decision.

Act 3: the resolution and the population health paradigm

Case: what has been decided so far

Case studyWho decides how many beds Romania has, Act 3

§A real case. The resolution is not a single decision, but a series of decisions, some still open. The National Health Strategy scheduled the revision of the national bed plan and divided responsibility among the Ministry of Health, the National Institute for Health Services Management, the public health directorates, the local authorities and the providers with beds4. The regional master plans have been finalised for all development regions32. The three regional emergency hospitals are under construction, with completion deadlines several years away67.

§What has not yet changed is the structure of the network: Romania still has more beds than the Union average, few rehabilitation beds and high avoidable hospitalisations2338. The answer to the question in the title is therefore this: the number of beds is decided by several actors, each for its own part, and none of them is solely accountable for the outcome for the population.

§The resolution leads us to the last topic of the module. If no actor is accountable for the outcome for the population, who should be, and according to which indicator? The question is no longer one about beds. It is one about the way the system defines its objective.

From treating disease to population health

§The textbook observes that, although the benefits of prevention and of “upstream” interventions, that is, those made before disease appears, have long been known, most of the planning effort has been directed towards treating diseases that are already established63. The explanation the textbook gives is one of incentives: payment systems have rewarded quantity, not quality, and have paid according to the volume of services63.

§The textbook links the current interest in planning to a paradigm shift: the move from medical care, narrowly defined as the set of functions under the influence of physicians, to the broader notion of health care, and the recognition that the dominant medicine of the United States, built on the old theory of disease, fits the new health problems less and less well64. These problems have their own patterns of illness: as social factors take the place of biological and genetic ones, the chronic diseases of civilisation are distributed selectively, according to income, education and other characteristics of groups, and the differences between groups become disparities65. The textbook describes American society; for Romania, a similar distribution across groups is a hypothesis to be tested in the data, not a finding of the textbook.

§The population health paradigm starts from the opposite direction. The unit of analysis is no longer the patient who has reached the hospital, but the population that a system, a region or an organisation serves, including the people who have not yet seen a doctor. The textbook describes the emergence of population health management as the applied form of this paradigm in the delivery of services and places the emphasis on group outcomes, not on the outcomes of each individual case6667.

§For our case, the paradigm shift has a precise consequence. In the disease-treatment paradigm, an occupied bed is a success: the hospital has activity, the insurance house has something to pay for, the community has a service. In the population health paradigm, some of the occupied beds are a failure: they reveal admissions that could have been avoided if chronic diseases had been cared for in time outside the hospital8. The same bed can therefore be read in two opposite ways, depending on the objective the system sets itself.

The attributes of population health management

§From the textbook's description, a few attributes can be drawn out that distinguish population health management from traditional care. We present them as a checklist that you can apply to any planning proposal. The list below is a synthesis made for the course, based on the textbook's chapter on population health, not the textbook's own list of attributes.

  • The population is defined in advance: you know who the people you are responsible for are, not only who came for a consultation.
  • The outcome pursued is the health status of the group, not the number of services delivered67.
  • Non-clinical contributions to health status, such as living conditions, education or behaviours, enter the plan alongside clinical ones68.
  • Interventions are chosen according to where they produce the greatest effect, and this is often outside the hospital. Health education and social marketing are activities suited to this approach69.
  • Providers coordinate, because the outcome for a group does not depend on a single organisation.

§Apply the list to the proposal from the role-play committee. The proposal defines a population (the region), pursues a group outcome (avoidable hospitalisations) and moves resources towards care outside the hospital. What it lacks, however, is explicit coordination of providers: who is accountable if a patient discharged from a rehabilitation bed cannot find a family doctor in their locality? The checklist does not give the answer, but it shows where the gap is.

The barriers

§If the paradigm is convincing, why is it applied with difficulty? The textbook identifies several barriers. One is that the usefulness of population health management is not immediately evident to administrators under pressure to deliver day-to-day care70. Another is the insufficient appreciation of the non-clinical contributions to health status: as long as health is seen as the result of medical care, interventions outside the health system appear not to be its business70.

§In Romania, the case of beds adds barriers of its own, which you saw in the first two acts. The decision is shared, so no one is accountable for the outcome for the population of a region1310. Planning capacity is officially acknowledged as insufficient52. And the system remains hospital-centred, with patients who go directly to hospital and bypass primary care38. Each of these barriers is, in fact, a question for planning: who is accountable, with what data and with what incentives.

Common misinterpretation

§A claim to check: “Population health management means fewer beds.”

§What the analysis shows: the approach starts from the needs and outcomes of the population served, and these may call for fewer acute care beds but more rehabilitation or palliative care beds3339. The total number of beds is a result of the plan, not its objective.

§General principle: a planning paradigm changes the question and the indicators; it does not fix the quantitative answer in advance.

The fifth decision: the first indicator you ask for

Applied activity 15 min

§Fifth decision (individual vote): you are a member of a county's planning committee and you may ask for a single indicator before judging the bed network. Which one do you ask for? (A) the number of beds per 100,000 population; (B) the bed occupancy rate; (C) the avoidable hospitalisations of the county's population, by condition, compared with other counties; (D) the average cost of a hospital day.

§The documented answer is option C. The number of beds per 100,000 population describes capacity, not need, as you saw in the pitfall in the second act. The occupancy rate tells how much the beds are used, but not whether they should have been used. The cost of a hospital day concerns the efficiency of the hospital, not the outcome for the population. Avoidable hospitalisations are an indicator of the population health paradigm: they link the hospital bed to the quality of care outside it and show where health is being lost that could have been gained earlier8. The comparison with other counties turns it from an isolated figure into a planning question.

§Note that option C does not exclude the other indicators; it puts them in order. A planner who starts with avoidable hospitalisations will then ask for the number of beds, the occupancy rate and the cost as well, but will read them through the question “what outcome does the population obtain?”.

The transfer: the same question, a different resource

§The case was about beds, but its structure recurs in almost every resource of the health system: imaging equipment, resident doctor posts, family medicine practices, ambulances. Each time, the same four questions arise. Who decides on the resource and who pays for its operation? Which planning document covers it, and over what horizon? Which indicator shows whether it serves the needs of the population? Who loses if the resource is moved?

Applied activity 10 min

§Transfer (individual): choose a resource from the institution where you work or the one you know best. Answer the four questions above briefly. Note the question to which you do not know the answer: this is, most probably, the point where planning is missing.

What you will do differently on Monday morning

§If you keep a single thing from this module, keep the question “who decides, and according to which indicator?”. It can be used immediately, without a planner's mandate.

§First, when you receive a proposal that calls for new resources, whether beds, equipment or staff, ask which planning document justifies it and which need of the population it covers; if the answer is “that is how we had it before”, the proposal is not yet planned. Next, when you read an aggregate indicator, such as beds per 100,000 population, look for its breakdown by type and by region before drawing a conclusion. Finally, when you take part in a decision shared among several actors, say explicitly who bears its cost and propose a compensatory measure before the actor who loses blocks the decision.

Review

  • Planning is the process through which a coordinated mechanism for decision-making and resource allocation is built; the plan is the framework for decision that results from it192022.
  • Health planning has a political nature: it often starts from a crisis or from an agenda and expresses someone's idea of how the system should look23.
  • In Romania, the number of beds is decided jointly by the hospital owner, which proposes the structure, the Ministry of Health, which approves the bed plan of each county, in accordance with the National Bed Plan approved by the Government, and sets the direction through the strategy, and the health insurance house, which contracts the beds within the limit of this plan1391614104.
  • Romania has more beds than the Union average, but few rehabilitation beds, an unequal regional distribution and high avoidable hospitalisations233348.
  • Discharges fell sharply during the pandemic, and beds did not follow activity; the Eurostat data for discharges and length of stay stop at 20214041.
  • Planning is carried out at three levels, national, community and organisational; in Romania, the community level of hospital planning is the development region, through the regional master plans4732.
  • The context of planning comprises the sociocultural framework, demographic trends, the attitudes of service users and the transformation of the health system; in Romania, population ageing changes the type of beds that are needed, not only their number545556.
  • The number of beds needed results from admissions, the length of stay and the target occupancy rate; a decision about beds is a decision about care outside the hospital.
  • The population health paradigm shifts the question from the volume of services to the health outcomes of the population served; population health management is its applied form in the delivery of care, with an emphasis on group outcomes6667, and its barriers are the pressure of day-to-day care and the underappreciation of non-clinical factors70.
Limits of interpretation
  • The status of the revision of the national bed plan: the framework contract refers to a National Bed Plan approved by Government decision16, and the strategy sets a deadline for its revision4; we could not verify whether the revised plan has been approved and published.
  • The bed occupancy rate and the average length of stay in Romania after 2021: the Eurostat datasets consulted stop in 202140 and do not publish the EU-27 average, and the dataset on occupancy has no data for Romania; for discharges, we have only the 2022 figures, from another series (OECD)42.
  • Recent changes to the financing of the regional hospitals through the European Investment Bank loan: could not be verified from the official source.
  • The EU-27 average for beds: the European Observatory and Eurostat publish different values for the same year, because of different sources and methods; in this module we use Eurostat.
  • A “correct” number of beds for Romania: there is no European norm that sets it, and the order-of-magnitude calculation in the second act is not a target.

Limits of interpretation
  • The status of the revision of the national bed plan: the framework contract refers to a National Bed Plan approved by Government decision16, and the strategy sets a deadline for its revision4; we could not verify whether the revised plan has been approved and published.
  • The bed occupancy rate and the average length of stay in Romania after 2021: the Eurostat datasets consulted stop in 202140 and do not publish the EU-27 average, and the dataset on occupancy has no data for Romania; for discharges, we have only the 2022 figures, from another series (OECD)42.
  • Recent changes to the financing of the regional hospitals through the European Investment Bank loan: could not be verified from the official source.
  • The EU-27 average for beds: the European Observatory and Eurostat publish different values for the same year, because of different sources and methods; in this module we use Eurostat.
  • A “correct” number of beds for Romania: there is no European norm that sets it, and the order-of-magnitude calculation in the second act is not a target.
